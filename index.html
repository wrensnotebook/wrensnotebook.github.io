Lab Session 01Teleportation, part 1

The Ground Is Moving

Say you step into a teleporter in California and step out in Russia. Clean, instant, whole — body and consciousness arriving together, no copy problem. There’s still a problem, and it’s not philosophy. It’s velocity.

Right now, sitting still, you are moving at a frankly irresponsible number of speeds at once. The ground under you is being carried east by Earth’s spin. The whole planet is falling around the Sun. The Sun is hauling the entire solar system around the center of the Milky Way. “Standing still” is something that only exists relative to the floor.

So when the teleporter asks “where should she appear?”, the honest follow-up is: relative to what? If it aims at a fixed point in space — a spot that stays put while Earth moves on — then during even a one-second jump, the destination pad has been carried hundreds of kilometers away from where your body materializes. You don’t arrive at the pad. You arrive where the pad used to be, which by then is empty sky, open ocean, or the inside of a mountain, depending on the second.

And it isn’t only your position that’s off — it’s your momentum. Arrive at a fixed point in space and you arrive still carrying California’s spin but not Russia’s, still moving with the Earth you left in some ways and not others. Best case, you’re flung sideways at hundreds of meters per second. This is why Skye’s middle-school conclusion holds up under scrutiny:

A working teleporter must be Earth-relative. It doesn’t send you to a place — it sends you to a place on a moving planet, and it has to hand you the planet’s motion, all of it, as part of the delivery.

Think of walking down the aisle of a moving train. You never notice the train’s speed because you never stop sharing it. A teleporter has to work the same way: your destination isn’t a coordinate, it’s a coordinate plus a ride.

Margin note

Skye’s verdict, having run the numbers with me: given the choice, she’d still rather fly. Flying never asks where “here” is. Hard to argue.

See it: the Jump Cam

Reading “260 km per second” and watching the ground leave are different experiences, so the lab built a camera. It rides with your molecules during the jump while the pad — faithful to Earth — drifts away underneath the motions you choose not to correct for. The odometer counts the miss in kilometers, and, because we could, in molecular diameters. (One water molecule is about 2.75 × 10−10 m across. A single second of untracked orbit puts 1.1 × 1014 of them between you and home.)

Two things this little machine makes obvious. First: with the Earth-relative lock on, nothing happens — no drift, no drama, you just arrive. Boring is what success looks like. Second: notice which toggle does the damage. Spin is a rounding error. Orbit is a problem. Galactic drift is an extinction-level rounding error. The universe’s biggest motions are the ones we feel least, because we’ve never once not been moving with them.

Session 01 conclusion: teleportation isn’t a distance problem, it’s a bookkeeping problem. The machine has to know, continuously and precisely, how its destination is moving — which raises the obvious next question. Moving relative to what? That’s Session 02, and it turns out a version of the answer is already orbiting overhead, keeping your phone’s maps honest.

Margins & Small Discoveries

Note · Light

The photon doesn’t get a frame

Every moving thing gets a reference frame — a point of view where it’s at rest. Except light. Ask “what does the universe look like from a photon’s perspective?” and physics answers: the question is broken. Light moves at c in every frame, which is exactly why frames had to be reinvented in 1905. Childhood-me would have lost her mind over this. Grown-me is losing hers politely.

Note · Flight

Why flying beats the jump

A bird — or a girl with wings, per the original childhood specification — never leaves the frame she’s in. Flight is continuous: every instant, you’re somewhere definite, moving at a speed you can feel. Teleportation outsources all of that to bookkeeping. Flying is honest travel. The jump is travel by accountant. (A very good accountant, if Session 02 goes well.)

Lab Session 02Teleportation, part 2Status: in progress

Frames All the Way Down

Session 01 ended on a hook: the teleporter must track its destination’s motion — but motion is only ever measured from somewhere. There is no master speedometer for the universe, no fixed point in space to anchor to. Physics gives us reference frames: choose a viewpoint, measure everything from there, and the laws work out the same. The teleporter’s real job, then, is choosing the right frame — Earth’s — and correcting for it relentlessly.

This is not hypothetical engineering. A working version flies over your head every day. GPS satellites orbit at about 20,200 km up, moving near 3.9 km/s, and their clocks misbehave in two directions at once: their speed makes them tick slow (special relativity, about −7 µs/day) while weaker gravity makes them tick fast (general relativity, about +45 µs/day). Net: roughly +38 µs/day — and if nobody corrected for it, GPS positions would rot by around 10 km every day. Your phone also has to account for the ground rotating underneath the signal while it’s still in flight. That is precisely the teleporter’s problem, wearing a uniform.

So Session 02’s working thesis: a teleporter is a GPS that moves people — pick the Earth’s frame, track it continuously, correct for relativity, and never, ever trust a fixed point. The full session is being written now; when it lands, it’ll live here, with the frame-switching worked example and the part where simultaneity gets strange.
